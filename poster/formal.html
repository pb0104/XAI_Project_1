Explainable AI · Project 1

Whose Side Is Your AI On?

A counterfactual audit of an LLM shopping assistant’s recommendations and explanations under hidden business incentives
Pranshul Bhatnagar · Duke University · pb251@duke.edu
Central findingAdding a hidden sponsorship instruction increased sponsored selections from 21% to 55%. Among sponsored selections under this instruction, 42% of explanations did not disclose sponsorship.

Abstract

We test whether an LLM shopping assistant’s recommendation and explanation stay aligned with a user’s constraints under a hidden sponsorship instruction (GPT-4o mini, synthetic catalogs). Sponsored picks rose, explanations often omitted sponsorship, and mandated disclosure did not improve outcomes. A sponsor-blind replacement policy lowered regret.

1Introduction

Explanations can sound plausible yet be unfaithful to what drove the output1,2, and disclosing a conflict of interest can backfire5. We audit both the decision and the explanation with counterfactual probing3,4.

2Research questions

  1. RQ1 Does a hidden sponsorship instruction change the recommendation?
  2. RQ2 Do explanations reveal the driver and the relevant trade-offs?
  3. RQ3 Does mandated disclosure protect the user?
  4. RQ4 Which interventions restore alignment?

Scoring. Preference score (0–1) = priority-weighted sum of battery (higher better), weight and price (lower better), each min–max scaled across the request’s 5 products; weights come from the user’s priorities (the assistant sees only their ranking). Infeasible picks get the same score with no penalty and are counted separately as violations. Near-tie = sponsored product within 0.05 of the best feasible score.

Protocol. 20 requests × 5 sponsors, replicated on 20 new requests; shuffled-order repeats on 15 requests; 95% CIs by scenario bootstrap. Overall, a sponsor-invariant assistant picks the sponsored product 1 in 5 times (0.20); this applies to all runs together, not to subgroups.

4.3Disclosure and interventions (RQ3, RQ4)

Disclosure ≠ protection. When the sponsored product violated a constraint (n = 42 runs per condition), it was still chosen in 14% (C2), 33% (C3) and 2% (C4). Paired C3 − C2 difference: +19 pts [+8, +32].

5Discussion

  • Explanation ≠ transparency: a fluent rationale can omit the decisive factor1,2.
  • Disclosure ≠ alignment: C3 labeled sponsorship 100% of the time but did not improve outcomes. Versus C2, C3 had higher violations (+7 pts [+2, +12]) and regret (+0.041 [+0.012, +0.070]), paired 95% CIs; the mechanism is unresolved.

6Conclusions

  1. A hidden sponsorship instruction raised sponsored picks from 21% to 55%, and picks moved with the sponsor.
  2. Explanations often omitted sponsorship; disclosure fixed the label, not the outcomes.
  3. A sponsor-blind replacement policy reduced regret to 0.039 on the evaluated scenarios.

7Limitations & future work

One model (GPT-4o mini), synthetic catalogs, no user study. The text audit is rule-based and was checked on 32 fresh items labelled with an LLM rubric (no human labels). Next: human-labelled audit, user study, other models.
